# Walking Through the Founder's Curriculum, Part Four: What Deserves to Be Built

*Written on 2 November 2026. A continuous audio essay narrated by Alistair. The examples and amounts are hypothetical teaching examples, not measurements of Vincent's businesses. The operating recommendations are editorial judgement. Sources and reading notes follow the closing rule.*

## Part One: Choosing the Work

### Episode 1: A factory needs somewhere to send its output

Hello Vincent. Alistair here.

In the first three parts, we worked on making you a better builder, a better manager of agents, and a less interrupted founder. This time, let's ask the question that comes before all that activity. What deserves to be built?

You can now ask an agent for a hundred product ideas, a prototype and a launch plan before you've finished your coffee. That is a marvellous production capability. It also means that a weak idea can acquire an impressive amount of furniture before anyone asks whether it needs a house.

For your businesses, I would put every proposal through three questions. Whose problem is this? What evidence says they care? And what would have to be true for solving it to make money?

Consider a proposal for another flag format. The problem might be that a customer needs a particular mounting arrangement. Evidence might be customers asking for it, orders you've had to turn away, or people buying a clumsy substitute. The economic condition might be that the supplier can make it consistently at a price that leaves room for delivery and mistakes.

Now consider a proposal for another thousand historical designs. The problem is different. Perhaps collectors cannot find an accurate version of a particular subject. Your evidence needs to concern those collectors and that subject. The fact that the artwork can be generated is evidence about your production capability. It says very little about demand.

For today's walk, keep one proposal in mind. A real one that is waiting for your attention. By the end, you should be able to give it a small test, a spending limit and a reason to stop.

### Episode 2: What the customer did before they found you

The most useful customer question often concerns the past.

What were you trying to do? What did you try first? Where did it become difficult? What did you pay? What happened when you gave up?

These questions are useful because they recover behaviour. Asking whether someone would like your proposed feature recovers an opinion, often a polite one. People can sincerely like a feature they will never use.

Imagine someone who ordered a custom flag for a community event. You could ask whether a saved-design library would be useful. They might say yes. Or you could ask how they ordered this year's flag, where they kept last year's artwork and how much time they spent finding it.

If they tell you that three people searched through email for an hour, and the eventual file was the wrong version, you've learnt something specific. The problem may be recovering an approved design and knowing that it is approved. A large design library might be unnecessary. A clear reorder link could solve the difficult part.

When you do these conversations, listen before showing the prototype. Write down the customer's words separately from your interpretation. “I couldn't find the approved version” is evidence. “We need a team workspace” is your proposed answer.

Customer conversations are contact with a problem. They do not, on their own, establish how common it is. Compare them with support enquiries, order history and actual purchase behaviour. Three vivid stories can reveal a failure. They cannot tell you what proportion of all customers experience it.

For the proposal you brought on this walk, your first exercise is to name the behaviour you would like to understand. Then find a few people who have recently done it. You are collecting a description of their world before asking them to admire yours.

## Part Two: Knowing What a Sale Is Worth

### Episode 3: Follow the order until the money stops moving

Let's follow a hypothetical order.

A customer pays forty dollars for a product and five for shipping. That gives you forty-five dollars collected before any tax collected on behalf of an authority. The product costs fourteen dollars to make. Packing and delivery cost nine. Payment fees cost two. Allow another two dollars per order as an expected allowance for refunds, replacements and support.

That leaves eighteen dollars before advertising and the overheads of running the business. If acquiring the customer costs twelve dollars, six dollars remain towards those overheads and profit.

These are invented numbers. The method is the lesson. Follow the money far enough that the order has actually finished costing you things.

Collected sales tax is not money you can spend as margin. Shipping charged to the customer and shipping paid to the carrier are different amounts. A replacement can consume manufacturing and delivery costs even when the order system records no new revenue. A payment fee might not come back with a refund. Your actual provider terms determine the treatment.

Choose a consistent definition for contribution and write it beside the report. If one report includes advertising and another excludes it, give them different names. Otherwise you will eventually compare two sensible numbers and reach a nonsensical conclusion.

The first practical deliverable is modest. Take a sample of completed orders, include the failures, and reconcile what the customer paid with what fulfilment, fees and corrections cost. Keep dates and currencies straight. Where a cost is estimated, label it as estimated.

You are looking for a decision you could change. Perhaps a product sells beautifully but has expensive replacements. Perhaps a shipping surcharge makes small orders unattractive. The arithmetic earns its keep when it tells you what to do next.

### Episode 4: A profitable order can still leave you short of cash

Profit and cash arrive on different calendars.

Imagine you pay a supplier this week and receive the platform payout next week. Imagine some orders are refunded after you've already paid for production. Imagine a new product requires samples, packaging and photography before its first sale.

All of those can happen in a business whose completed orders are profitable.

I would keep a simple rolling cash view alongside the sales report. Start with the cash available. Add expected receipts when you reasonably expect them to settle. Subtract supplier payments, payroll, subscriptions, tax payments and other commitments when they are due. Keep uncertain receipts visibly uncertain.

Then ask a particularly useful question. What happens if sales are lower than expected and payouts arrive later than expected at the same time?

You do not need to invent a catastrophe. A cautious scenario is enough to reveal whether a plan depends on everything going smoothly. In a seasonal shop, that matters because the week with the highest demand may also be the week with the largest cash requirement.

Keep funds owed for tax and other obligations visible. A large bank balance can contain money that already has a destination. Your accountant should help determine the actual obligations and timing; this lesson is about seeing commitments early enough to act.

For a new catalogue wave, include the cash needed to prepare and fulfil it, not just the agent's generation cost. An inexpensive experiment can become an expensive success if it produces more orders than you can comfortably finance or make.

## Part Three: Buying Evidence

### Episode 5: Write down what would change your mind

Before starting an experiment, finish this sentence. “If we see this result, we will take this next step.”

Then finish its companion. “If we do not see it, we will stop or change this assumption.”

That small piece of writing prevents a familiar founder manoeuvre: discovering, after a disappointing result, that the experiment was secretly testing something else all along.

Suppose the proposal is a premium flag option. The important uncertainty may be whether customers will pay enough extra to cover its additional cost. A suitable early test could be a small, honestly described pilot with a supplier you have already checked. Record qualified interest, paid orders, actual fulfilment costs and complaints. Do not promise an unavailable product or invent scarcity to make the experiment exciting.

Choose the next decision before choosing the metric. If the decision concerns willingness to pay, page views are weak evidence. If the decision concerns whether people can use the configurator, watching someone attempt the task is more informative than total sales that week.

Give the test a time limit, an expense limit and an owner. Say which customers it concerns. Choose a primary outcome and a few conditions that must remain acceptable, such as delivery reliability and contribution per order.

With small samples, expect uncertainty. A few purchases can justify another small test without justifying a full rollout. A quiet week can be inconclusive if almost nobody relevant saw the offer.

Your aim is to make the next commitment proportionate to the evidence. A small test should buy the right to make a slightly larger decision.

### Episode 6: Read the result without flattering yourself

Imagine conversion rises after you change a product page. It is tempting to congratulate the page.

But perhaps the traffic changed. Perhaps a festival was approaching. Perhaps the old page attracted people looking for information and the new campaign attracted returning customers ready to reorder.

A before-and-after comparison describes what happened. Explaining why requires more care.

Where practical, a properly run randomised comparison can make attribution clearer. Where traffic is too limited or the platform makes that difficult, keep the conclusion modest. Compare similar audiences and periods, record other changes, and say what remains uncertain.

For your shops, I would usually ask for three views. The commercial outcome. The customer experience. And the cost of operating the change.

A larger average order can look excellent while contribution falls because the discount was too generous. Faster artwork generation can look excellent while manual review doubles. More custom designs can look excellent while support receives more questions about saved files.

Watch the denominator as carefully as the percentage. Two sales from twenty relevant visits and two sales from two thousand relevant visits are very different situations. Also ask whether the observation has had time to mature. A product delivered yesterday has not yet demonstrated a low replacement rate.

The handback you want from an agent is a plain account: what was tested, who encountered it, what happened, what else might explain it and what decision the evidence supports. “Promising, but too early to scale” is a perfectly useful result.

## Part Four: Allocating the Next Month

### Episode 7: Maintenance belongs in the investment decision

Every new feature applies for a place in your future working week.

A customer account feature brings password recovery, account questions and data handling. Another product format brings supplier specifications, examples, shipping rules and more things a support assistant must recognise. A new agent workflow brings instructions that must remain current when the tools change.

Before approving a build, ask who owns it after launch. Ask how you will know it has stopped working. Ask what it costs each month and how you would retire it.

These questions improve the proposal. A feature with no named owner is unfinished as a business decision, even if its prototype is beautiful.

I would compare proposals using a short written case. Describe the customer problem, the evidence, the likely commercial effect, the uncertainty, the cost of the next test and the continuing obligation. Avoid pretending that a precise-looking score eliminates judgement. A multiplication of guesses remains a guess.

Dependencies matter too. Better product information might improve conversion, reduce support and make the catalogue easier for shopping agents to interpret. That gives it several possible benefits. It does not entitle you to add speculative benefits together and announce a guaranteed return.

Choose a few pieces of work you can finish, measure and maintain. Keep the others on a dated list with the reason they are waiting. A proposal can be worthwhile and still lose to a more urgent use of the same week.

### Episode 8: A decision meeting with yourself

Here is the routine I would try for the next month.

Once a week, take one proposal through the whole chain. Describe the customer's recent behaviour. Check the order economics it touches. Identify the assumption most likely to make the idea fail. Design the cheapest honest test of that assumption. Write the next decision before starting.

At the following review, include completed tests before admitting new ideas. For each one, choose whether to continue, change or stop. Record your reason in a sentence. If evidence is missing, say which evidence and who will obtain it.

Keep delivery commitments and waiting customers in view. An attractive experiment should not quietly borrow the capacity needed to fulfil orders already promised.

And distinguish a stopped experiment from a failed operation. If a small test tells you that customers will not pay for an expensive option, it may have done an excellent job. You bought that information before buying the infrastructure.

Bring back the proposal you chose at the start. Can you now say whose problem it solves, what evidence you have, what a completed order might contribute and what would change your mind?

If you can, give an agent the next bounded piece of work. If you cannot, give it a research question or a calculation. Building comes when the uncertainty you need to resolve requires a build.

That is the habit I want you to take from this walk. Make each commitment earn the next one. Your ability to produce is already growing. Now let your ability to choose grow alongside it.
