# Walking Through the Near-Peer Propaganda Poster Webshops

*A continuous audio essay. Researched and written on 14 October 2026. Competitor facts come from each shop's own About page, public Shopify product feeds and listing pages, all read on 14 October 2026, plus one UK Companies House record. Our own figures come from the current VPP-woocommerce repository. The comparisons, sizing and recommendations are our own and their assumptions are stated. A dated source list closes the script.*

*The brief: Vintagraph and the other shops that sell vintage propaganda posters online. When were they founded, how big are they, how do they price, and how does our Vintage Propaganda project (VPP) size up against them? This episode builds on the market walk of 5 October (kept in the VPP-woocommerce repository's `docs/`), which first met Vintagraph. It adds founding dates, a wider set of near peers, a refreshed crawl and a direct comparison with the WooCommerce build.*

## Part One: Who Counts as a Near Peer

### Episode 1: The Short List

Welcome to Walking Through the Near-Peer Propaganda Poster Webshops.

In the market walk on 5 October we sorted the vintage poster world into six different businesses. Auction houses and original dealers sit at the top. Museum shops, marketplaces and free archives sit around the edges. This walk is about the middle: independent webshops that sell reproduction prints of historical propaganda, with a catalog of their own, a brand of their own and a checkout of their own. Those are the shops a customer would compare us with in a second browser tab.

We settled on four.

Vintagraph is the established general poster shop with a large propaganda section. Propagandopolis is the specialist: an archive and research project with a print shop attached. Vintage Poster Archives is a very new British company with a war and propaganda collection. The Poster Archive is an adjacent shop that sells public-domain posters next to its own invented ones. It does not specialise in propaganda, so we treat it as a design benchmark, not a rival.

We left out marketplaces such as Etsy, Fine Art America, Redbubble and Amazon. They matter, and the earlier episode covered them, but they are channels with thousands of anonymous sellers, not peers with a founding date and a voice. We also left out original-poster dealers, who are a different customer on a different price scale.

One more boundary. "Near peer" is our label for shops of a similar kind and size to what we are building. It is not a claim about sales. We have no traffic, order or revenue data for any of these shops. Everything below is about what they list, charge and say about themselves, not what they sell.

### Episode 2: What "Founded" Can and Cannot Tell Us

Before the dates, a warning about them.

A founding year is a claim a shop makes about itself, or a date on a registry. It is not the date a shop first sold anything online, and it is not the date its current website went live. Shopify also stamps each product with a creation date, which people sometimes mistake for founding. We use those dates as a hint about when a catalog was built, never as the founding date itself.

So for each shop we give the source of the date and say how firm it is. Where we could not find one, we say so.

## Part Two: The Shops

### Episode 3: Vintagraph, Founded 2009

Vintagraph's own About page says it has been restoring and selling custom fine-art posters and prints since 2009. It says it prints on museum-quality paper with archival inks, restores images from high-resolution scans, and specialises in travel, First World War, Second World War, WPA and fruit-crate prints. It also says the images come from a mix of material the team owns and public sources such as the Library of Congress, and that every image goes through digital restoration. The same page says Vintagraph is run by the team behind Shorpy.com, the historic photo archive. [Vintagraph About Us](https://vintagraph.com/pages/about-us).

An older page on Shorpy describes Vintagraph as a site started by Juniper Gallery, originally to sell reproductions of 1930s WPA posters on French art paper. We saw that only as a search summary, and it is an older page, so we treat the origin story as plausible but unconfirmed. [Shorpy on Vintagraph](https://www.shorpy.com/node/3209).

That makes Vintagraph about seventeen years old, the oldest shop on our list by a wide margin.

One wrinkle. In the public product feed of its propaganda collection, the earliest listings are stamped 2012, and 124 of the 250 listings on the first page carry that year. The About page says 2009. We think the likeliest reading is that the business predates its current Shopify store, but that is our inference, not something either source says.

Now the size. Our crawl of Vintagraph's public product feeds on 14 October 2026 found 288 products in its propaganda collection, split across two pages of 250 and 38. The whole shop holds 1,576 products. Both numbers match the crawl we took on 5 October exactly, so nothing has moved in nine days. The propaganda products have an average of 40.9 variants each. That is the Product, Style and Size option grid in action, with paper prints, canvas prints and framed prints in several sizes. Across the entire shop the feed lists 63,375 variants.

On price, Vintagraph's cheapest variant is 20 dollars. The median across the propaganda collection is 31.95 and the maximum is 329.99. For the Keep Calm and Carry On poster the art-paper ladder runs 20 dollars at 8 by 10 inches, 35 at 11 by 17, 60 at 16 by 20, 110 at 24 by 32 and 250 at 30 by 40. Canvas runs 98 to 310 dollars. Black or brown framed prints run from 169.99 for 8 by 10 up to 329.99 for 16 by 20. [Vintagraph propaganda collection](https://vintagraph.com/collections/propaganda-posters).

What does that tell us? Vintagraph is a general retailer with a deep catalog, a long history and a wide format menu. Its propaganda section is large but it is one aisle in the shop, and it leans heavily American, as the 5 October episode found.

### Episode 4: Propagandopolis, Established 2018

Propagandopolis describes itself as an online archive, research project and print shop dedicated to the history of propaganda. Its About page says it was established in 2018, and that its founder, Bradley Davies, has published a book with FUEL called Propagandopolis, in 2024. Most of its posters are reproductions, though it says it occasionally sells originals. It also says every print purchase supports the wider project, and that the project is neutral: "we are enthusiasts interested in the history of propaganda." [Propagandopolis About](https://propagandopolis.com/pages/about).

This is the nearest match to what we want VPP to be: a specialist with a point of view, a research identity and a print shop that funds the research. It is eight years old.

Its public product feed, read on 14 October 2026, lists 707 products with 2,062 variants. Most products offer three sizes, named Small, Medium and Large, and a few add framed options. Prices run from 9.99 to 109.99 in the shop's feed currency. The median is 14.99. A typical Soviet poster is 9.99 at Small, 14.99 at Medium and 27.99 at Large. Search listings show both pound and dollar prices, so we are not sure which currency the feed uses, and we do not convert it. [Propagandopolis prints](https://propagandopolis.com/collections/frontpage).

The creation dates in the feed climb from 73 products stamped 2019 to a peak of 162 in 2023, then 92 in 2024, 38 in 2025 and 41 so far in 2026. We read that as a catalog that was built quickly, then slowed. Again, that is a hint, not a fact about sales.

Two other points. First, Propagandopolis sells with a book behind it. The FUEL book is stocked by the Poster House museum shop, which gives the brand a credibility that a pure webshop lacks. [Poster House shop listing](https://shop.posterhouse.org/propagandopolis-a-century-of-propaganda-from-aroun.html). Second, its prices are low and its size ladder is short. It is not trying to sell a frame or a statement piece. It is selling the poster.

### Episode 5: Vintage Poster Archives, Incorporated May 2026

Vintage Poster Archives is the newest shop on our list, and the closest in age to us.

Its website carries the legal name Vintage Poster Archives Ltd and a company number. The UK Companies House record shows a private limited company incorporated on 19 May 2026. That is less than five months before today. [Companies House record](https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/company/17229210).

The caution matters here. A company's incorporation date is not the date its shop began trading. The shop may have existed before it was incorporated, or begun trading later. We could not find a dated launch. So the firm fact is the registry entry, and the founding year of the brand itself is unconfirmed.

On the site, the shop says it sells museum-quality poster reproductions produced and shipped from the UK, the US and Canada, on 200 gsm archival paper with pigment-based inks. Its war and propaganda collection lists 188 products, spread across the 1910s to the 1970s. It covers First and Second World War posters from Britain and America, Soviet propaganda from the 1920s onwards and Chinese propaganda art. The 1940s dominate with 87 products, then 25 from the 1910s, 25 from the 1960s, 21 from the 1950s and 16 from the 1970s. The site runs on Shopify. [Vintage Poster Archives, war and propaganda](https://vintageposterarchives.com/collections/war-propaganda).

What stands out is the tone and the tooling. The homepage describes its prints as "Beautiful prints. Questionable influences," and it runs editorial pieces such as one on the Second World War campaign that was scrapped in four weeks. It also offers a See It On Your Wall preview that lets a customer view a poster at scale on their phone. We did not see that feature on the other shops we read. We did not capture its prices, so we make no price comparison.

For us, this is the shop to watch. It is new, it has chosen almost exactly our subject, it already has a voice, and it has built a buying aid that we have not.

### Episode 6: The Poster Archive, the Design Benchmark

The Poster Archive is not a propaganda shop. We include it because it shows how a young independent presents trust.

Its About page says it focuses on early and mid-twentieth-century design and presents two kinds of work: real public-domain reproductions, and its own invented posters, presented as artifacts from imagined cultural history, such as fictional exhibitions and films. It says plainly that the in-house works "are never presented as historical artifacts." It describes an in-house review and restoration step, and says every print is made to order by Gelato, a print-on-demand network operating in 32 countries, with production in two to three business days. It says its prints are priced "for living spaces, not auction houses." [The Poster Archive About](https://theposterarchive.com/pages/about-the-poster-archive).

We found no founding date. We include it for the honesty of its labelling. A shop that separates real from invented in its own About page is giving its customers exactly the kind of clarity that a propaganda shop needs, and we will return to that in the positioning section.

### Episode 7: Others We Saw but Did Not Profile

A few more names appeared in our searches, and it is fair to list them.

Heritage Posters, a UK shop, sells First World War reproductions such as Britain Needs You At Once, with listing prices from 4.50 to 18 pounds and sizes from A4 to A1. We did not read its About page and found no founding date. [Heritage Posters listing](https://www.heritage-posters.co.uk/product/britain-needs-you-at-once-print/).

Authentic Vintage Posters and Collect Russia sell originals, not reproductions, at prices from about 35 dollars to over 1,500. They are a price ceiling, not a rival.

The Imperial War Museum shop and the Poster House shop are institutions. We covered them on 5 October.

## Part Three: How We Size Up

### Episode 8: Where VPP Stands Today

Now the honest part. Here is what VPP is as of the current repository, and what it is not yet.

The catalog has 491 curated poster designs. Each has fifteen valid combinations of paper, frame and size, giving 7,365 variants. In the curated set, 104 designs are recorded as American, 73 as British, 32 as Soviet, 18 as German, 13 as Canadian, five as French and one as Austrian. The country field is not filled for 245 designs, so any claim about the geographic mix of the whole catalog has to wait. There is also a request-only archive of 4,970 further records, which are editorial entries and not products you can buy.

The build is WooCommerce on WordPress with a native Printful connection, replacing the paused Medusa project. All the code is in place, but the shop is not live and not release-ready.

That matters for every comparison below. As of 8 October, the compiler reports zero release-ready products. All 491 descriptions are drafts until checked against the image and a second source. EUR prices are pending, with a proposed matrix and no approved figures. Print files are not approved. The first pilot is ten designs, not 491. So when we compare catalog sizes, we are comparing what is built, not what a customer could buy today.

Our own start date is simple. The market research began on 5 October 2026 and the WooCommerce refactor began on 8 October. We are days old.

### Episode 9: Size Compared

Here is the catalog comparison in one place. All figures are what the shops list, read on 14 October 2026, except ours.

Founded: Vintagraph says 2009. Propagandopolis says 2018. Vintage Poster Archives was incorporated on 19 May 2026, with the shop's launch date unconfirmed. The Poster Archive gives no date. VPP began in October 2026.

Propaganda or related catalog: Vintagraph has 288 products in its propaganda collection and 1,576 in the whole shop. Propagandopolis has 707 products across the whole shop, which its own description frames around propaganda. Vintage Poster Archives has 188 products in its war and propaganda collection. VPP has 491 curated designs, plus the 4,970 request-only archive records.

Variants per design: Vintagraph averages 40.9 variants on its propaganda products. Propagandopolis averages 2.9. VPP has 15.

Price range: Vintagraph runs from 20 to 329.99 dollars. Propagandopolis runs from 9.99 to 109.99 in its feed currency. VPP's proposed matrix, still pending approval, runs from 18 to 94 euros, with the unframed range from 18 to 47.

Platform: Vintagraph, Propagandopolis and Vintage Poster Archives all run on Shopify, according to their public product feeds and site code. VPP runs on WooCommerce.

Reading across, three things are clear.

First, on propaganda-specific catalog depth, our 491 designs sit above Vintagraph's 288 and Vintage Poster Archives' 188, and below Propagandopolis' 707. But our 491 are not yet for sale, and the other three can ship today.

Second, we are deliberately narrow on formats. Fifteen variants per design is far fewer than Vintagraph's forty-one, which are mostly the multiplication of canvas, framing and size. The market walk called this the merchandise trap. Fewer formats means less work, fewer errors and a cleaner catalog, but it also means a smaller menu.

Third, the price ladder. Propagandopolis proves there is a market at the bottom, with prints starting under ten units. Vintagraph proves there is a market at the top, with a single large art print at 250 dollars. We would sit in the middle.

### Episode 10: Price Compared

Prices need care because the currencies, sizes and papers differ. We compare on similar sizes where we can, and we say where we cannot.

Our pending proposal gives a 21 by 30 centimetre matte print at 18 euros, 30 by 40 at 24, 50 by 70 at 30 and 70 by 100 at 43. Those numbers come from a cost-plus calculation with a 35 percent contribution target, Printful base costs recorded in dollars on 7 October, and an illustrative exchange rate. They are not approved and they do not measure what customers will pay.

Vintagraph's nearest size to our largest is its 30 by 40 inch art-paper print at 250 dollars. In centimetres that is about 76 by 102, slightly bigger than our 70 by 100, and our proposed price for the latter is 43 euros. Even allowing for the extra size and the difference in currency, the gap is wide. Vintagraph's price also includes a long brand history and US fulfilment, so lower is not automatically better. A very cheap poster can read as a cheap poster.

At the small end, Vintagraph charges 20 dollars for 8 by 10 inches. We propose 18 euros for a bigger 21 by 30 centimetre sheet. Propagandopolis, at 9.99 for a Small, undercuts both.

Framed prints are where the gap is largest. Our proposed black or oak frame prices run from 43 euros for 8 by 10 inches to 94 for 18 by 24. Vintagraph lists framed prints from 169.99 dollars for 8 by 10. We have not verified that the frames are comparable in material or finish, and we should not claim they are.

What we conclude from the prices: there is room to charge more than the cheapest shop and well below the dearest, but pricing alone will not win. Cost-plus prices say nothing about the value of restoration work, which is the thing we should be selling.

### Episode 11: How the Shops Talk

Numbers aside, the shops sound quite different.

Vintagraph is a catalog. Its About page talks about archival inks and hanging the art on its own walls. It says little about the subject matter or the history of what it sells.

Propagandopolis is a research project. It says it is neutral, calls its customers enthusiasts and puts the study of propaganda ahead of the shop.

Vintage Poster Archives is wry. "Beautiful prints. Questionable influences." It publishes short stories about individual posters and offers a try-on-your-wall tool.

The Poster Archive is careful. It labels real and invented posters separately and says so.

Our voice, set out in the project's instructions, is a measured British museum enthusiast: facts first, short paragraphs, warm understatement, and no invention. Founder pages use Vincent's first person. That sits closest to Propagandopolis in seriousness and to Vintage Poster Archives in dryness. It is distinct from Vintagraph's neutral catalog tone.

The point is that voice is one of the few things a new shop can differentiate on without a large catalog or a long history.

## Part Four: Positioning

### Episode 12: Where We Can Win

Four openings come out of this comparison.

One, a complete war. The 5 October walk found Vintagraph's First World War section overwhelmingly American, with some British and Canadian. Our curated catalog also leans that way, with 177 of the 246 designs that have a recorded country being American or British. So the opening is not there yet. It is a gap we can close on purpose, by weighting the next thirty designs toward French, German, Austrian, Italian and Russian posters. Propagandopolis already covers many countries, so we would not be alone, but its angle is breadth, not curation.

Two, restoration shown, not claimed. Every shop on our list says it restores. None of the pages we read showed a before and after. A side-by-side of a low-resolution web copy and our restored file would be evidence rather than a slogan. That is the cheapest differentiator we have.

Three, European metric sizing and fulfilment. Vintagraph prices in inches and dollars. Vintage Poster Archives serves the UK, US and Canada. Propagandopolis' currency is unclear to us. A shop priced in euros, sized in centimetres and fulfilled from Europe is not an obvious feature of any of the four. This one is worth confirming, because Printful's European production is a supplier claim we still need to test.

Four, honest labelling. The Poster Archive's separation of real from invented, and our own rule that every print is described as a modern reproduction, are the right instinct for a subject where trust and context matter. The project's instruction to say plainly when an artist or printer is unknown is a strength, not a weakness.

### Episode 13: Where We Are Weak

The risks are just as clear.

We are not live. Every comparison above is a catalog we could have against shops that can ship today. The distance between a built catalog and a trusted shop is large. Draft copy, pending prices, unapproved print files and an unconnected Printful store are the work between here and there.

We are new in a category where age counts. Vintagraph has seventeen years of reviews, search history and brand recognition. Propagandopolis has eight years and a published book. The only shop in our age bracket is Vintage Poster Archives, and it already has an editorial voice and a preview tool.

We are in the category's search results nowhere. We have no backlinks, no reviews and no traffic data.

And the demand data is missing for everyone. We do not know what any of these shops sells. The catalog counts show effort, not revenue. Propagandopolis' slowing product additions, from 162 stamped in 2023 to 38 in 2025, could mean saturation, or just that it chose to stop adding. We should not read it as either.

### Episode 14: What to Do Next

Six steps follow from this.

One. Keep the pilot at ten designs and finish the approvals before widening it. Our advantage depends on quality, and a rushed launch gives that away.

Two. Choose the next thirty designs deliberately for countries the near peers cover thinly, and record the country for the 245 designs that have none.

Three. Build a restoration before-and-after into each product page and into the About page.

Four. Treat Vintage Poster Archives as the competitor to track. Re-read its collection count, prices and editorial output monthly. Its Companies House record gives a clean date to anchor that.

Five. Decide on a wall-preview tool. It is a feature a new rival is already using. Whether it is worth building is a separate cost question.

Six. Repeat the catalog crawl before launch. The numbers in this episode are a snapshot of 14 October. Vintagraph's did not move in nine days, but a young shop's can.

That is the walk through the near-peer shops. The oldest was founded in 2009, the specialist in 2018, and the newest was incorporated this spring. We are the youngest of all and, in catalog depth, not the smallest. The distance that matters is from a catalog to a shop customers trust.

Thank you for listening.
