# Walking Through 《大展鸿图》

> **Read this first: five corrections to the original briefing.**
> 1. **The song is identified.** It is 《大展鸿图》 by 揽佬 SKAI ISYOURGOD with 刘夫阳. "攬佬" in the brief is not a transcription error. It is the artist's name.
> 2. **It is not mainly a Cantonese rap.** Chinese media describe it as Mandarin with a Huizhou / Hakka-flavoured accent ("广普"), plus some Cantonese. The artist says dialect appears only "a little".
> 3. **The 道/盗 pun is not Cantonese-only.** The two characters are homophones in Mandarin too (both *dào*), so the pun works in either language. The brief's claim that it only works in Cantonese is wrong. This one is my own linguistic knowledge, not from a source I fetched.
> 4. **Machine translation did not "treat it as Mandarin" wrongly.** Mandarin is the base language. The translator's real trouble is the Cantonese and Guangzhou slang dropped into it.
> 5. **I could not open the YouTube link** (`bCjKdnvZDok`). I matched the song from the lyric content and place names. I did not confirm that the video ID is this song.
>
> **Lyric policy:** following the brief, this piece only glosses single words and very short phrases. It does not translate the song line by line.

# PART 1: SPOKEN SCRIPT (about 20 minutes)

## Cold open

Picture a rap song that opens with a crackly Cantonese opera sample, as if drifting out of an old uncle's radio. Then a cheap, lo-fi drum machine kicks in. Then a villa, a karaoke room, a silver fish in the pool.

A year after it came out, that song knocked Jay Chou off the top of Spotify's Chinese-language chart. Most people who streamed it could not understand the words. Their comment of choice was: "Understanding 0%, Vibing 100%."

Today we find out what it is actually saying.

## Who made it

The artist is 揽佬. In English the stage name is SKAI ISYOURGOD. The song is 《大展鸿图》, "Da Zhan Hong Tu," with a guest verse from 刘夫阳. It sits on the album 《八方来财》, which came out on 20 August 2024.

揽佬 was born in 1998 in Huizhou, a city in Guangdong. The father is from Leshan in Sichuan and the mother is from Guangdong, so the household spoke Mandarin. That matters later. 揽佬 studied performance at the Sichuan Communication College, worked as a crew actor, a livestream seller and an electric-company assistant, and tried several music styles before settling on Memphis rap. That is according to the Sohu feature and the Chinese Wikipedia entry listed at the end.

揽佬 does not give a real-name interview in most coverage, though Wikipedia and Sohu both print it. We'll stay with the stage name.

One more detail. In an interview with The Paper (澎湃), 揽佬 says: "我就是个打工的，不是什么艺术家", roughly "I'm just a worker, not an artist." Keep that tone in mind. It helps explain why the song is funny and not grand.

## The language question

Let's settle what language this is, because the brief had it wrong.

Tencent News calls the style "客家口音的普通话及粤语", Mandarin with a heavy Hakka accent, mixed with Cantonese. The Paper calls it "带着客家口音的'广普'". 广普 means Guangdong-accented Putonghua. Huizhou Daily says "夹杂着惠州口音的广普" and describes the style as Mandarin rap with Hakka vocabulary and Cantonese intonation.

In a later interview, 揽佬 says dialects appear only a little in the songs, and the priority is that "很多人听懂我在想什么", that many people understand what they are thinking. That line comes from a Sina summary I flag below as AI-generated, so treat it as a lead and not a firm quote.

Cantonese does show up. The second verse includes a short stretch of proper Cantonese, with grammar and particles that a Mandarin speaker would not use. So "a Mandarin rap with Cantonese passages" is the accurate description.

This matters for the reception story. Some Guangdong listeners said the song was too crude, and that it was sung in Putonghua anyway, so it could not represent Guangdong culture. Tencent's report on the split reaction says exactly that, and asks why a song about Guangdong isn't sung in Cantonese, Hakka or Teochew.

## The pun at the centre

Now the pun everyone asks about. The line is "黑灰蓝白道亦有道". The idea behind it, 盗亦有道, is very old: even thieves have a code.

The idiom is traditionally traced to the Zhuangzi, in the chapter 《胠箧》. A follower asks the famous robber Zhi: "盗亦有道乎?", does a robber have a way too? The robber answers yes, and lists five virtues a good thief needs: sagacity, courage, righteousness, wisdom and fairness in dividing the loot. The sources I found describe it as the "Cutting Open Satchels" chapter.

In the song, 道 (the way) and 盗 (the robber) are homophones. Both are *dào* in Mandarin and both are *dou6* in Cantonese. So the line works either way. The rapper gets to say "there's a way inside the way," and a listener hears "there's a code among thieves."

The "black, grey, blue, white" colours name kinds of paths to money. I have not found a source that decodes the four colours in this song, so I'll leave the colours as colour and not guess.

## Layer one: the opera

Back to the opening. The sample comes from the Cantonese opera 《帝女花》.

According to Tencent News, the song samples 《帝女花》, and the music video says it commemorates the 68th anniversary of its first performance. The Sohu academic feature identifies the sampled stretch as the opening passage of the opera, the one that sets a scene of mist over a fairy mountain, and says it is not the better-known aria people hum.

The facts about the opera itself, from the sources I found: it was written by 唐涤生 (Tong Dik-sang), and first performed in 1957 by the 仙凤鸣 troupe, founded by the two stars 任剑辉 and 白雪仙. It is set at the fall of the Ming dynasty and tells of Princess Changping and her husband-to-be Zhou Shixian. One source dates the premiere to June 1957, another gives a different month, and the Sohu piece dates the film to 1959, so I'll say 1957 for the stage premiere and leave the month alone.

Here is the interesting tension. Several Guangdong commenters pointed out that the song has nothing to do with the opera's plot. The opera is about a princess choosing death and loyalty to her fallen dynasty. The song is about a villa and a luxury watch. Tencent reports this as a main criticism.

Counter-voices argued that "阳春白雪" and "下里巴人", high art and folk art, are not enemies. So the sample works as an invitation to a particular place and age, not as a plot reference.

## Layer two: the flex

Next, the flex. The chorus is the best-known stretch of the song, so I'll describe it and not quote it.

A rich uncle, a villa, a pond with a silver arowana, and a gift of tea things. In return, the uncle grinds ink and writes four characters: 大展鸿图, an auspicious phrase wishing someone great plans fulfilled. Then the calligraphy goes up in an office, and the god Guan Gong, patron of loyalty and also of business, approves.

The Sohu feature reads this as a Guangdong young person's first look at the business world: a wealthy uncle, the fish that stands for luck, and a gift hoping for a career. It calls the tone "有点搞笑，又有点嘲讽", a little funny and a little mocking.

Huacheng Metropolitan Daily (West China) reads the arowana differently. For the Southeast Asian world it is a symbol of wealth, and for Western listeners it is an exotic pet, so people from different backgrounds read the same image differently. I'd treat that as one outlet's interpretation.

## Layer three: the code

Now the code of the *jianghu*, the world of rivers and lakes.

The lyrics circle the same few ideas: be more afraid of insulting a gentleman than a petty person, treat your brothers right, and mind that luck cannot stay at the top. And "人情软过玻璃", favours are softer than glass, a line the Sohu feature picks out as a lesson about money, loyalty and luck.

Two phrases are worth glossing, but only as far as the sources let me.

"贼船越大老鼠才坐的下": the bigger the pirate ship, the more rats it can carry. One lyric commentary I found reads it as "think big", but I could not confirm that against a primary source, so I'll leave it as a reading.

"收米": the brief marks this as "collecting money". My searches did not return a source defining the term, only related slang such as 收片 for protection money. So: unresolved. I will not claim it means protection money in this song.

Then, "劳力士还是AMG", a Rolex or a Mercedes-AMG. Status goods you would take a risk for.

Worth knowing: the album sits in a style the critics call "揽式孟菲斯", Lan-style Memphis. Memphis rap is a 1990s style from Tennessee built on cheap drum machines, lo-fi horror-film samples and repetitive hooks. The critic 邹小樱 says the lyrics work like plain subject-verb-object, fast symbols that tell you at once what is being said, a bit like absurdist humour. The artist told The Paper that the gear is cheap and the mix was done in about half an hour.

## Layer four: Guangzhou as home

The second verse swings to Guangzhou, and each place is checkable.

**越秀 (Yuexiu)** is the old city core. **小北 (Xiaobei)** is a district in the north of Yuexiu, named in the lyric alongside 淘金 (Taojin), another old area. I could not source the claim in the brief that "gold mining" refers to trading communities, and I would not say it.

**骑楼 (qilou)** are arcade-style shophouses, common in Guangzhou and Hong Kong.

**海印 (Haiyin)** is a Guangzhou area. The lyric has the rapper carrying a box of 打口 CDs on the shoulder. 打口 literally means "saw-gash": Western CDs that were surplus or damaged stock, notched and exported to China, and in the 1990s a main way Chinese listeners reached Western music. English-language coverage from Time and Upworthy describes the trade and says the CDs were bought cheaply and resold in Guangzhou. The link to the Haiyin market comes from the lyric itself. I did not find a source on the Haiyin market's own history.

**九爷鸡 (Jiu Ye Ji)** is a real roast-meat takeaway on Guangzhou's Wenming Road, a tiny shop with a Hong Kong café style, according to one travel listing. One lyric commentary says it opened in the 1980s as the city's first roast-meat fast-food place, but I found no source for that, so I leave it unverified. The brief marked this one LOW, and I have moved it to "real place, history unverified."

**太平馆西餐厅 (Tai Ping Koon)** is real. Chinese Wikipedia traces it to a cook named 徐老高 and gives 1860, though other sources say 1885. It describes Guangzhou's original Taiping Sha restaurant, the Beijing Road branch and the Hong Kong branch founded by the family. The song says it "moved but the spirit remains." The sources describe several moves and closures, so I cannot say which one the song means. I'd call it a lament that old-school places survive in spirit.

Then the food: 啫啫煲 (sizzling claypot), 凉茶 (herbal tea), 赛龙舟 (dragon boat racing), 叉烧包 (barbecue-pork buns), and 碱水粽 (alkaline-water rice dumplings). And a joke: "中华小当家", the Chinese title of the cooking anime *Cooking Master Boy*. That is my own knowledge, not a fetched source.

And the stretch about 任剑辉 and 白雪仙, the opera stars again, followed by a line about being Cantonese and standing up. The opera stars are named in the lyric, and that is the clearest sign the song wants tradition and hip-hop in the same breath.

The line about the time-honoured brand that never changes its taste is the thesis of that verse. I'd read it as the rapper's local-pride statement.

## Layer five: the Warcraft sword

"霜之哀伤" is the Chinese name of Frostmourne, the cursed sword from Warcraft III. I'm confident of that from general knowledge, though I did not fetch a Blizzard source. The "妖魔革命" it is pointed at I could not resolve. It may be a lyric-transcription quirk or a gaming or anime reference. Unresolved.

## How it blew up

Time for the numbers, with dates.

The song came out in August 2024 and was not an instant hit. The Sohu feature credits a May 2025 dance video by 不齐舞团, and ETtoday reports that the troupe is from Chongqing and says it was the first to choreograph and register the dance. The dance is simple: arms as a bird spreading its wings, a foot slide, and a head flick. Tencent and West China Metropolitan Daily both report more than 500,000 Douyin remakes.

Abroad, it started in overseas-student circles and went to foreign creators through TikTok, not through mainstream media.

On 7 July 2025, Spotify's chart showed 揽佬 with about 3.022 million monthly listeners, and Jay Chou with about 2.975 million, per Sohu and Tencent. Chinese Wikipedia says the music video, shot at Happy Valley Racecourse in Hong Kong with a champion jockey, came out on 24 June 2025. Tencent also says the shoot took in the Lin Heung Lau teahouse.

Later figures: Spotify monthly listeners passed 4.36 million by early August per Sohu, and about 5 million per The Paper, a peak I could not confirm from Spotify itself.

## Pushback

Tencent describes the split reaction: foreigners enjoyed the hook, and many Guangdong people found it tacky or objected to the Mandarin. Some online comments called it a victory for "low culture."

揽佬's own reply, from Tencent: 土 and 潮, tacky and trendy, are words people made up, and anything that exists and has an audience has its reasons. In Huizhou Daily: "土潮结合那不刚好吗?" Tacky plus trendy, isn't that just right?

The critic 伍福生 warned that the real challenge is avoiding being a flash in the pan.

## Where it stands now (as of 6 October 2026)

According to a Tencent piece dated 13 September 2026 and Chinese Wikipedia, Sony Music China announced on 10 September 2026 that 揽佬 had signed a worldwide exclusive deal with RCA Records China. The same piece says overseas tours in New York, Los Angeles, Toronto, Tokyo, London and Paris sold out, short-video views for the two hit songs exceeded 4.5 billion, and YouTube views on related videos passed 67 million.

A Sina article says the EP 《八方来财之江船入海》 won best rap album at the Wave Music Awards on 6 September 2026. That article is labelled "content generated by AI" on the page, and I found no second source, so treat the award as unconfirmed.

A search summary also mentions a performance at the Beijing Grand Canal music festival on 5 October 2026. I did not open a page for that, so it is unconfirmed too.

## Close

So, is it gibberish? No. It is a Mandarin-with-accent rap, built on a Memphis beat, wrapped around an opera sample, packed with Guangzhou place names and food, and aimed at the oldest question in the jianghu: what is honour worth when money talks?

Whether it is a proud portrait of Guangdong or a cartoon of it depends on who you ask. Guangdong listeners disagree with each other. That argument is part of the story.
