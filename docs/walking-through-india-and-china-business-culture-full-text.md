# Walking Through Indian and Chinese Business Culture

*A continuous audio essay in two episodes, narrated by Claudine. Researched and written on 25 October 2026. Episode one is about working with Indian programmers. Episode two is about working with Chinese factories, and in particular dye-sublimation textile printing, the process behind printed flags. The backbone is a handful of respected books, supported by blog posts and columns from people who have done the work: consultants, engineers, inspectors and lawyers. Outside claims carry a named source, listed below the closing rule. Where something is my own judgement, I say so.*

*One warning before we start. Both episodes generalise about countries of well over a billion people each. Every source I used says the same thing in its own way: these are tendencies, not rules, and the person in front of you matters more than the map.*

## Episode 1: India, and the programmers

### Hello, and a confession about the books

Hello, Vincent. Claudine here.

This is a deep dive on working with Indian business culture, aimed squarely at one situation: hiring, managing or partnering with software developers in India. Agencies, freelancers, a contracted team, or eventually a small team of your own.

I'll start with a confession about the books, because you asked for recent, highly respected ones, and India is where that's hardest. The two books every practitioner cites are not new. The first is Erin Meyer's The Culture Map, from INSEAD, published in 2014 and still the standard text on cross-cultural business. The second is Craig Storti's Speaking of India, first published in 2007 and later updated with material on managing remotely. Practitioners still recommend Storti, often with a sigh about its age. I looked hard for a newer book of the same standing focused on Indian workplace culture, and didn't find one. What has appeared recently is history: an oral history of the Indian IT industry called Against All Odds, from 2022, edited by Kris Gopalakrishnan, one of the founders of Infosys, with two co-authors. It tells the industry's story in the words of the people who built it.

So the backbone of this episode is Meyer and Storti, sharpened by people writing from the field: a consultant called Neil Miller who works in India and writes the Learning India blog, a cross-cultural trainer writing a column for CIO magazine, Martin Fowler of Thoughtworks writing about his company's Bangalore offices, and the industry press on how Indian tech employment actually works.

### The scale of what you're dealing with

First, some context, because the Indian software world isn't one thing.

There are the giant IT services companies: Tata Consultancy Services, Infosys, Wipro, HCL and others. They employ engineers by the hundreds of thousands and rent them out to clients worldwide. There are the global capability centres, which are offices that foreign companies own and run themselves. By Wikipedia's count, citing industry figures, there were about seventeen hundred of them in twenty twenty-five, employing nearly two million people in cities like Bengaluru, Chennai and Hyderabad. Then there's a fast-growing product and start-up scene. And there's a vast freelance market.

The culture you'll meet differs a great deal between those worlds. A senior engineer at a product company in Bengaluru may behave much like an engineer in Amsterdam. A junior developer at a large services firm, trained in a strongly hierarchical organisation, often won't. Storti himself is careful about this. Neil Miller points out that Storti limits his remarks to educated urban professionals in the private sector, and even within that group the variety is enormous.

Keep that in mind for everything that follows.

### The famous "yes"

Now the thing every Western manager mentions first: the yes that doesn't mean yes.

The CIO column puts it memorably. Its author, a cross-cultural trainer, draws on years of work with the Swissair Group, Credit Suisse and Novartis. Time and again, someone would ask some version of, "How can I make my associates in India say no?" One banker told the author that after a run of bad experiences, he now treats the phrase "no problem" as an alarm. "No problem," he said, "means there is a serious issue and it requires immediate attention."

Why does this happen? The column's explanation is about hierarchy and face. In many Indian organisations, superiors and subordinates follow clearly hierarchical principles. "The employee avoids contradicting his superior," the author writes, "so that neither of them loses face." A yes to an instruction from above isn't a promise. It's a way of keeping the relationship intact.

Meyer makes the same point about hierarchical cultures generally. If you tell people your view first and then ask what they think, everyone will feel obliged to agree with you. She describes the difference between a real yes and a polite one: "Yes, we will try" may simply be politeness. If it comes with hesitation, or a sigh, she says, it probably means no. That example comes from her advice about China, but she places India in the same broad group: high-context communication, where the listener reads between the lines, and a much thinner line between business and personal life.

And then there's the head movement. Meyer describes the half-nod, half-shake that many Indians use, and the way it confuses Westerners on video calls. It doesn't signal disagreement. It usually signals interest, attention or respectful listening. On a grainy video call, a Western manager can easily misread it as doubt.

### How Indians say no

Storti's most useful contribution is a list of the ways Indians commonly say no without using the word. Miller summarises it: a delayed response; asking what you think; a conditional yes; and hesitation. So "I'll try my best", "Let me check and revert", "It should be possible if the API is ready", and a long pause before agreeing are all, potentially, a no.

There's a lovely insight in Storti that Miller expands. Every culture has its own scale of indirect, direct and blunt, and the scales don't line up. Miller gives an example of refusing a boss's wedding invitation. What an Indian would consider plainly direct, "It would be a real honour, but I have an important family commitment that day, though I will try to come", sounds to a German like an indirect, half-hearted maybe. What a German considers direct, "Unfortunately I won't be able to come", sounds to many Indians close to rude. So when you ask an Indian colleague to be more direct, they may be thinking, "I am being direct!"

### Not all Indians are indirect

Here Miller adds a correction that I find genuinely useful. Indians, he writes, can be very direct and very indirect, depending on how delicate the relationship is.

The most delicate relationships get the most indirect treatment: bosses, top clients, and foreigners. Peers and mid-level clients usually get direct communication. And subordinates, support staff and small vendors often get blunt treatment. One visiting manager was shocked to see a man she thought very meek shouting at his subordinates in front of her.

Why are foreigners on the delicate list? Partly hospitality. Miller cites the Sanskrit phrase that the guest is god, and the many stories told to children in which a visiting guest turns out to be a god in disguise. His example is a left-handed foreign manager who spent a whole trip handing things over with his left hand, which is considered impolite. Not one of his team corrected him. Only a shopkeeper, who saw him as an ordinary customer, finally did.

The practical lesson is uncomfortable but important. As a foreign client and a boss, you're at the top of the delicacy scale. You are the person least likely to hear bad news directly. Your job is to move yourself down that scale over time, into the peer category, where people feel safe telling you the truth.

### Command and control, and the Thoughtworks lesson

Martin Fowler's long essay on offshore agile development is the most thoughtful first-hand account I found from a software company. Thoughtworks has run large offices in India for years. Fowler wrote the essay in the mid two-thousands and said that a 2011 visit confirmed the lessons still held.

His section on culture is frank. Many companies, he writes, run on command and control, "which assumes that seniors make decisions and lower level people carry them out." Agile methods need the opposite: autonomy and decision-making by the people doing the work. He sees the problem in Western companies too, but finds it amplified where cultures reinforce deference to superiors. He describes a training course at a major Indian contracting company that defined management as "the science of control". In that environment, he writes, people are often discouraged from asking questions, raising problems, warning about impossible deadlines, or proposing alternatives.

His advice: "Beware that polite acceptance is often a sign of an important issue not getting discussed." And be careful not to sound authoritative, because that reinforces the pattern. The bad news, he says, is that changing it is an uphill battle and takes longer than you'd think. The good news: "Once people realize they have the freedom, and the responsibility, of making decisions, they seem to really relish it." His Indian colleagues told him their friends at other companies couldn't believe how much autonomy they had.

He also records, honestly, that even writing about this caused friction. Some Western analysts who read a draft called the section patronising. One of his developers in Bangalore said he was being far too mild. That tension is itself worth knowing about.

### What works: the practitioners' toolkit

Pulling the sources together, here's the practical toolkit.

First, ask open questions, never yes-or-no ones. Not "Can you finish this by Friday?" but "What would need to be true to finish by Friday?" and "What's most likely to slip?" A yes-or-no question invites the polite yes.

Second, speak last. Meyer's advice for hierarchical teams is to avoid giving your view first. Ask for theirs, or better, let the team discuss without you and report back. She suggests leaving the room for fifteen minutes and asking the group to come back with their view.

Third, make bad news safe and cheap. Thank people visibly when they raise a problem early. Fowler's teams use short iterations, regular builds and short daily status meetings precisely so that problems surface in days, not months. In the language of our own series: give the work a check it can run. A failing test can't be polite.

Fourth, write things down. Fowler recommends wikis for shared information and test scripts to make requirements concrete, and admits that offshore work needs more documents than co-located agile. Written specifications, acceptance criteria and examples remove a great deal of guessing.

Fifth, invest in the relationship. Fowler's most distinctive practice is the ambassador: someone from the home team who spends weeks or months at the Indian office, and someone from India who spends time with the home team. Part of the ambassador's job, he says, is to pass on gossip, the informal context that never makes it into a ticket. "The plane fares soon repay themselves in improved communication." For a small company, the equivalent is a visit, regular video calls with cameras on, and time spent on things other than the task.

Sixth, small talk is not small. Miller has a whole post on it. In India, he writes, small talk is an investigation. People want to know about your family, your work, where you studied, so they can place you and find ways to help you. Family is the best topic of all; cricket, film stars and travel within India are good ones. He lists topics to avoid: skin colour, caste, and condescending remarks about Indian customs or about people's English. On that last one he points out that India's English proficiency is among the highest in Asia.

### Indian English, briefly

A small but real source of confusion is Indian English, which is a full variety of the language with its own usage. A few examples you'll meet in emails: "do the needful", meaning please take whatever action is needed; "revert", meaning report back; and "prepone", meaning bring forward, the opposite of postpone. None of it is wrong. It's simply a different standard, and treating it as an error is one of the quickest ways to lose goodwill.

### How the employment market shapes behaviour

Now the structural side, which explains a lot of behaviour that looks cultural but isn't.

Attrition in Indian IT has historically been high. The Register reported in twenty twenty-two that the four biggest outsourcers averaged nearly twenty-three per cent attrition over the previous year, from about seventeen per cent at TCS to nearly twenty-eight at Infosys. Rates have come down since, but sources disagree on the current numbers, so I won't give you one.

Notice periods are long. Large services firms commonly require sixty to ninety days, according to several Indian HR sources, while product companies and capability centres tend towards thirty to sixty. That matters when you hire: your new developer may not start for three months.

And services firms have a "bench": people between client projects. Industry reporting describes bench periods shrinking and firms letting benched staff go. For you as a client, the consequence is simple. On a services contract, the people you're given may be junior, may rotate, and may leave. The Register's own conclusion in twenty twenty-two was that clients would "likely be served by less experienced staff".

### What this means for us

Let me finish with the practical takeaways for how you work.

First, if you hire developers in India, hire people, not headcount. A named senior engineer you can talk to directly is worth several anonymous juniors on a services contract. Ask who exactly will do the work, and ask what happens when they leave.

Second, design the work so the truth comes out by itself. Short tasks, written acceptance criteria, a demo or a passing test at the end of each. That's the same advice we read in the Claude Code manual about agents, and it works for exactly the same reason: it doesn't rely on anyone volunteering bad news.

Third, ask open questions, speak last, and thank people publicly for raising problems early. Move yourself, over months, from honoured foreign client to trusted peer.

Fourth, put in the human time. Ask about family. Visit once if you can. Remember the festivals; Diwali in particular is a major holiday, so plan around it.

And fifth, remember what Fowler found. Give people autonomy and responsibility, and many of them take it with real enthusiasm. The culture you build in your own team counts for more than the culture you inherit.

That's India. Next, China, and the factories.

## Episode 2: China, factories and dye-sublimation printing

### Hello again

Hello, Vincent. Claudine here, for the second episode.

This one is about working with Chinese manufacturers, and in particular about printing: the dye-sublimation process that puts colour into polyester flag fabric. China is the centre of the world's textile and printing supply chain, and if you ever source fabric, printed flags or finished goods from there, the cultural and practical lessons here apply directly.

The books are more recent this time, and better. Patrick McGee's Apple in China, published in twenty twenty-five, is the most talked-about business book on Chinese manufacturing in years. McGee was the Financial Times's main Apple reporter, and the book draws on more than two hundred interviews. Dan Wang's Breakneck, also from twenty twenty-five, is a widely praised account of China as what he calls an engineering state. And Paul Midler's Poorly Made in China, from two thousand and nine and updated in twenty eleven, is older but remains the classic first-hand account of a small importer's life with Chinese factories. Midler coined the phrase "quality fade".

Around those, I've used the people who deal with factories every day: Renaud Anjoran, a certified quality engineer who has worked in Chinese manufacturing since two thousand and five and runs the QualityInspection blog; the China lawyers at Harris Sliwoski, whose China Law Blog has been required reading for importers for many years; the journalist James Palmer, who wrote a famous essay on "close enough" culture; and David De Cremer, who wrote on trust for the Harvard Business Review.

### Why China, still

Start with McGee's argument, because it explains why China remains so hard to replace.

Apple, he argues, concentrated its manufacturing in China because China was the only country that could offer enough labour at a low enough price, and, crucially, with a flexibility and speed nobody else could match. Reviewers keep quoting the phrase "China speed". Apple didn't just buy capacity. It poured in machinery, engineers and training on an enormous scale, squeezed its suppliers on margin, and in doing so helped build the skills of China's whole electronics industry. Eric Seufert, reviewing the book, calls it a Faustian bargain for both the company and the country.

Dan Wang offers the deeper reason. China, he writes, is an engineering state that can't stop itself from building. And he has a line that every importer should remember: "Process knowledge is hard to measure because it exists mostly in people's heads and the pattern of their relationships to other technical workers." That's why a supply chain can't simply be moved. The know-how lives in clusters of people and firms who've solved the same problems thousands of times.

Wang's book isn't a celebration, by the way. Hollis Robbins's review rightly stresses its long chapter on the one-child policy, a reminder that the same engineering approach can also crush people. The working conditions in Apple's supply chain are part of McGee's story too.

### The cluster you'd deal with

For printed textiles, the cluster is easy to name. Keqiao, a district of Shaoxing in Zhejiang province, south of Shanghai, is described by Wikipedia as one of the largest textile dyeing and printing bases in China, with a vast fabric market. A sourcing agent who took a client there in late September describes it as heavily focused on textiles, unlike the general-goods market at nearby Yiwu, and notes a point that matters for us. Cotton needs a different printing process and machinery from polyester and blends. Dye-sublimation is a polyester process: the dye turns to gas under heat and bonds into the fibre.

So when you talk to a Chinese print supplier, you're talking to someone embedded in one of the densest clusters of fabric and print know-how on earth. That's the opportunity. Now the traps.

### Trust starts at zero

David De Cremer's Harvard Business Review piece gives the frame. In the West, he says, the default position is trust: you give the benefit of the doubt until something breaks it. In China, the default tends to be distrust: a wariness until someone shows they deserve trust. His advice is to invest real time in building personal relationships, even when it's frustrating, because once you're deemed trustworthy, "things will move very quickly." And trust, once built, tends to last.

Erin Meyer says much the same. Business and personal life are far less separate in China. The managers who succeed, she says, get to know people at a deeper level, often through long dinners where business is never mentioned. "Colleagues in other countries need to know and trust you before they can do business with you."

The Chinese words you'll hear in this context are guanxi, roughly the network of relationships and mutual obligation, and mianzi, face. Academic reviews describe causing a partner to lose face as one of the surest ways to damage the relationship. In practice that means: criticise in private, praise in public, and give people a way to fix a problem without being humiliated.

### The unsaid no, again

Meyer's advice to a Western manager sent to run a Chinese factory sounds very like last episode's advice on India. His predecessor had failed because the team kept doing things the old way and nobody mentioned major production problems until it was too late.

Her suggestions: understand that a Chinese boss is expected to be more like a parent figure, involved in people's lives. Learn to recognise the unsaid no: "If you ask a Chinese employee, 'Can you get this done in two weeks?' he or she will say something like, 'Yes, we will try.'" With a sigh or hesitation, she says, that probably means no. And because group consensus matters more than individual opinion, don't put individuals on the spot in meetings. Let the group confer and report back.

For a buyer dealing with a factory, the same applies. "No problem" and "we can do" are where the conversation starts, not where it ends.

### Quality fade

Now Midler's famous idea, in his own words as Anjoran quotes them: quality fade is "the deliberate and secret habit of widening profit margins through a reduction in the quality of materials." The first production sample is fine, but with each run, a little more of what should be there goes missing. The changes are subtle and progressive, so importers rarely notice.

Midler's book is full of related patterns. Reviewers and an economics lecture on his account highlight the low opening price to win the order, followed by price increases just after the importer has committed to a big customer and can't switch, and the sense that signing a contract is only the first step of the real negotiation.

How do you defend against it? Anjoran quotes an electronics specialist who gives the answer I find most convincing: make it very clear that you're able to walk away. Own the whole design, including the specification, the files and any tooling. Document every point you'll check on every shipment, so there's no wiggle room. And Anjoran adds that the owner matters. Factory bosses focused only on making deals, who spend no time in their own operations, tend to tolerate quality fade. Forward-thinking owners who run their shop floors don't. Put that in how you choose suppliers.

### Close enough

There's a cultural word that sits behind a lot of this: chabuduo, which means "close enough". James Palmer's essay in Aeon describes it as "a job seventy per cent done, a plan sketched out but never completed, a gauge unchecked". He traces it back to a satirical story from nineteen twenty-four about a Mr Chabuduo who lives his whole life by the principle. And he notes its other face: the brilliant improvisation of people who kept machines running for decades with no spare parts.

The China Law Blog's take is more pointed. It defines chabuduo as prioritising doing things quickly over doing them correctly. Its author gives a string of client stories: shirts with uneven stitching and discolouration, where the factory suggested the buyer just cut the price and sell them anyway; two shipments of licensed clothing in the wrong colour, both times because the factory confused two schools with similar names. Twice. The firm's remedies are the same every time: due diligence on suppliers, detailed written specifications, testing, inspections, and China-specific contracts written in Chinese.

I'd add my own note. Chabuduo isn't laziness or bad faith. It's a reasonable response to a world where speed is rewarded and nobody has said exactly what "correct" means. The fix is to say exactly what correct means, in writing, with numbers.

### Protecting your designs

For anyone sending artwork to a Chinese printer, the China Law Blog's advice on contracts is essential. A Western-style non-disclosure agreement, it argues, does little in China. Instead, use what it calls an NNN agreement: non-disclosure, non-use and non-circumvention.

Non-disclosure is the familiar part. Non-use is often the most important: the factory may use your designs to make your products, and for nothing else, not for itself or other customers. Non-circumvention stops the factory going around you to your own customers, which matters for companies whose value lies in design, branding and customer relationships rather than patents. That's us.

Two practical points stand out. First, identify the Chinese company by its registered Chinese legal name, not an English trade name, which the lawyers call "virtually meaningless in China" because hundreds of firms may share it. Second, write the contract in Chinese and English, with the Chinese version controlling, because a Chinese court will work from the Chinese text either way. The firm also warns against accepting a factory's own "mutual" NNN, which often quietly drops the clauses that matter most.

### Inspection: verify before you pay

Anjoran's simplest rule is the most important: once the goods arrive and you've paid in full, there's very little you can do. That's why professional importers inspect before shipment.

The standard pattern is a final inspection at the factory once production is complete and most of it is packed, with samples drawn at random so nothing can be hidden in a back room. Leave a few days between inspection and shipment so the factory can fix packing or labelling. Hold back the balance payment until the inspection passes. Anjoran notes that most disputes arise because the buyer never defined clear specifications in the first place.

And plan around the calendar. Chinese New Year shuts most factories for weeks, not days. Sourcing firms warn that many workers don't return afterwards, that factories take weeks to get back to full capacity, and that both the pre-holiday rush and the post-holiday restart are high-risk times for quality. Order early, and inspect harder around the holiday.

### Specifying a printed flag

Now let me bring it down to the product. Applying all of this to dye-sublimation printing, here's what a written specification should cover. This list is my synthesis of the supplier data sheets and print-trade advice I read, not a single source.

The fabric: knitted polyester, by weight in grams per square metre; one hundred and ten or so is common for single-sided flags. Name the exact fabric, not just "flag fabric".

Print-through: how much of the image shows on the reverse of a single-sided flag. Suppliers quote figures like eighty to ninety per cent for heavier knits. Agree a number and check it.

Colour: ask for the factory's colour profile, approve a physical strike-off on the actual fabric under agreed lighting, and set a measurable tolerance. Print-trade advice warns that a CMYK file sent to a factory without colour management gives unpredictable results, and that Pantone references only help for genuine spot colours.

Durability and safety: colourfastness and lightfastness for outdoor use, and fire-safety certificates where the market needs them. Data sheets cite standards like B1 and NFPA 701. Ask for the certificates themselves, not a line in a listing.

Finishing: hems, stitching, sleeves, grommets and their positions, with photographs of an approved golden sample.

And packing, labelling and the inspection standard, all written down before the order is placed.

### What this means for us

Five takeaways.

First, relationships are the strategy, not the decoration. Plan for slow trust-building at the start: visits, dinners, small first orders, and paying on time. Once trust exists, the speed is extraordinary.

Second, never let "no problem" end a conversation. Ask open questions, give people private ways to raise problems, and never make a supplier lose face in front of others.

Third, write down what "correct" means. Every quality problem in these sources starts with a vague specification. For printed goods: fabric, print-through, colour tolerance, durability, finishing, packing, all in numbers, with a golden sample.

Fourth, keep the power to walk away. Own your artwork and specifications, use an NNN agreement written for China, inspect before the balance payment, and keep a second supplier warm.

And fifth, respect the know-how. Dan Wang is right that process knowledge lives in people. The best Chinese print factories know more about dye-sublimation than almost anyone in Europe. Treat them as experts you're learning from, hold them to a clear standard, and you get the best of both.

That's the deep dive, Vincent. Two very different countries, with one surprisingly similar lesson: the polite yes is the start of the conversation, and the structures you build, written specifications, checks that can fail, relationships that make the truth safe, are what turn culture from a risk into an advantage.
