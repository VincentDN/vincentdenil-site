# Walking Through the Law of Selling Propaganda Poster Reproductions

*A continuous audio essay. Researched and written on 7 October 2026. Every claim is tied to a named source and, where the source gives one, a date. Where I could not confirm something from a primary source, the narration says so. A source list closes the text.*

*The brief: we want to sell reproductions of historical propaganda posters, from the World Wars, the Soviet Union, the Second Republic of Spain, the American home front and so on, from a small webshop run out of Belgium and shipping across the EU and beyond. What can we legally do, what can we not do, and which court cases tell us where the lines are?*

*This is research, not legal advice. Before launching anything with Nazi or other extremist symbols in it, a lawyer in each target market should check the plan.*

## Part One: Three Separate Questions

### Episode 1: Why "is it legal?" is really three questions

Welcome to Walking Through. Today's question sounds simple: can we print and sell copies of old propaganda posters?

It isn't one question. It's three, and they have nothing to do with each other.

The first is copyright. Somebody drew that poster. Is the drawing still owned by someone? And is the scan we want to print owned by someone else, like a museum?

The second is criminal and public-order law. Some symbols on those posters, the swastika above all, are restricted in some countries. Not because of who owns them, but because of what they stand for.

The third is private rules. Etsy, eBay, Amazon, Shopify and PayPal each have their own policies. They can shut a shop down for things that are perfectly legal.

A poster can pass one test and fail another. A 1917 American recruiting poster is free of copyright and carries no restricted symbols, so it passes everywhere. A 1943 Soviet poster by an artist who died in 1985 has no legal symbol problem in most of the EU, but it is still under copyright. A 1930s Nazi party poster by an anonymous designer may be free of copyright and still be a criminal matter if we ship it to Germany or Austria.

So we take the three questions one at a time. Then we add a short fourth part on consumer and product rules, and end with a plain list of what we can and cannot do.

## Part Two: Copyright, Who Owns a 1942 Poster?

### Episode 2: The basic EU clock

Start with the rule everything else hangs on. In the EU, copyright in an artwork lasts for the life of the author plus seventy years. That's Article 1(1) of the Term Directive, Directive 2006/116/EC. The clock runs to the end of the calendar year, so an artist who died in 1950 is free from 1 January 2021.

There's a second clock for anonymous work, and propaganda is full of it. Article 1(3) of the same directive says that for anonymous or pseudonymous works, protection runs for seventy years after the work was lawfully made available to the public. A poster published anonymously in 1944 is therefore free from 1 January 2015. One caveat: if the author's identity is revealed during that period, the normal life-plus-seventy clock applies instead. "Unsigned" is not always the same as "anonymous". Many unsigned posters have well-documented designers.

There is a third rule for works from outside the EU, called the comparison of terms. Article 7(1) of the Term Directive says that when the country of origin of a work is outside the EU and the author is not an EU national, protection in the EU expires when it expires in the country of origin, but never later than the EU term. In plain words: a foreign poster is never protected longer in the EU than at home, and never longer than life plus seventy.

### Episode 3: Running the clock on real posters

Let's run that clock on real artists, because this is where the money and the risk are.

Soviet posters first. Dmitry Moor died on 24 October 1946 and Viktor Deni on 3 August 1946, according to the National Library of Israel's authority file and Wikipedia's biography of Deni. That puts both in the EU public domain from 1 January 2017. Gustav Klutsis died in 1938, so he has been free since 2009. That date is from general knowledge, not a source I fetched for this episode.

Now the traps. Alexander Rodchenko lived from 1891 to 1956, per the Spencer Museum and Eastman Museum records. His work is still protected in the EU through 31 December 2026 and only becomes free on 1 January 2027. That's three months from today.

Irakli Toidze, who drew the famous 1941 poster *The Motherland Calls!*, died in 1985, according to the Georgian National Museum's artist page. His most famous poster is protected in the EU until the end of 2055. It's everywhere online, but legally it is not free to print and sell.

German posters run on the same clock. Ludwig Hohlwein, the great poster designer whose late work includes Nazi-era commissions, died on 15 September 1949, per Wikipedia and the city of Wiesbaden's lexicon. His work has been free since 2020. Hans Schweitzer, who signed his Nazi propaganda posters as "Mjölnir", died on 15 September 1980. His work is protected until the end of 2050. So of the two most famous Nazi-era poster artists, one is public domain and one is not.

### Episode 4: American, British and the grey zones

American posters are usually the safest, for three separate reasons.

First, as of 1 January 2026, every work published in the United States in 1930 or earlier is in the US public domain. Duke University's Center for the Study of the Public Domain confirmed this on its Public Domain Day 2026 page. James Montgomery Flagg's 1917 "I Want You" Uncle Sam poster is therefore free in the US. Flagg was American and the poster was first published in the US, so under Article 7 it cannot be protected longer in the EU either.

Second, many later American posters were published without a copyright notice, which before 1978 usually meant no copyright at all. J. Howard Miller's "We Can Do It!" poster, made for Westinghouse in 1943 and hung in its plants for two weeks that February, is the famous example. The US National Archives' DocsTeach page and Wikimedia describe it as public domain because it was published without a notice.

Third, works of the US federal government have no copyright in the US. That's 17 U.S.C. § 105, which covers many posters from the Office of War Information. There's a wrinkle, though. The legislative history of § 105, quoted in the Cornell and govinfo editions of the statute, says the rule "is not intended to have any effect on protection of these works abroad". So the US government may claim copyright in other countries. Under the EU comparison of terms there's a good argument that the EU protects these works no longer than the US does, which is not at all. But I'd call this a grey zone, not settled law. In practice, enforcement against reproductions of 1940s US government posters is rare. Treat that as an observation, not a guarantee.

British posters run on Crown copyright. Section 163(3) of the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 says Crown copyright in an artistic work lasts 125 years from creation. If the work is published commercially within 75 years, it instead lasts fifty years from the end of the year of publication. A Ministry of Information poster published in 1940 was therefore out of Crown copyright by the end of 1990. The catch: not every British wartime poster is a Crown work. Artists commissioned as freelancers may have kept their copyright, and then the normal life-plus-seventy clock applies. Check who drew it and on what terms before assuming it's free.

### Episode 5: The scan is a separate question

Suppose the poster itself is free. The next question is whether the scan we print from belongs to someone.

For years in Germany, the answer could be yes. On 20 December 2018, Germany's Federal Court of Justice (BGH) decided the Reiss-Engelhorn Museum case, I ZR 104/17. A Wikipedia volunteer had uploaded museum photographs of public-domain paintings. The court held that the museum's photographs enjoyed the German related right for simple photographs, the "Lichtbildschutz" of § 72 of the German Copyright Act, even though the paintings themselves were free. It also enforced the museum's photography ban in its house rules.

The EU then changed the rule. Article 14 of the Digital Single Market Directive, Directive (EU) 2019/790, says that when the term of protection of a visual artwork has expired, material resulting from a reproduction of it is not protected by copyright or related rights unless the reproduction is itself original. Germany implemented this as the new § 68 of its Copyright Act, in force since 7 June 2021, per the German Bundesrat's 2021 reform documents.

Belgium did something different. It did not transpose Article 14 at all, and explained why in the explanatory memorandum to its 2022 transposition law, Chamber document 55 2608/001, pages 12 to 13, as quoted by Europeana. Belgian law already protects only original creations, so a faithful, non-original reproduction of a public-domain work was never protected in Belgium in the first place.

So the scan of a public-domain poster is normally free to use in both countries. But two things survive.

The first is contract. If we downloaded a high-resolution file under terms that say "no commercial use", we agreed to those terms. That's a contract claim, not a copyright one. The Reiss-Engelhorn ruling already showed courts will enforce a museum's house rules. The second is any original creative work someone added, like a heavy restoration or recolouring.

The clean approach is to work from scans published with no restrictions, or from originals we own and scan ourselves, and to keep a record of where each file came from.

### Episode 6: Trademarks, the trap nobody expects

Copyright expires. Trademarks don't have to.

The cautionary tale is "Keep Calm and Carry On". The poster was printed by the British government in 1939 and mostly never issued. According to Wikipedia's summary of the trademark history, a company called Keep Calm and Carry On Ltd, run by Mark Coop, registered the slogan as an EU trademark, number 009455619, after failing to register it in the UK. It then issued takedown requests against other sellers. A cancellation action filed in July 2012 by the UK firm Trade Mark Direct dragged on for more than a year, per that firm's own blog posts from 2012. Wikipedia reports the cancellation was rejected. The EU mark finally expired on 18 October 2020, and the US registration was cancelled on 13 July 2018.

The lesson is that a public-domain image can still be blocked by somebody's trademark in a slogan or a design element. Before printing a famous slogan, search the EU (EUIPO), Benelux (BOIP) and national registers.

The opposite lesson comes from a 2011 case at the EU's General Court, Couture Tech v OHIM, case T-232/10, decided on 20 September 2011. A company tried to register the coat of arms of the Soviet Union as an EU trademark. The court upheld the refusal, holding that a mark must be refused if it is contrary to public policy in even one member state. Hungarian law treated the hammer and sickle and the red star as symbols of despotism. That doesn't stop us selling Soviet posters. It does mean we cannot build a protected brand on those symbols in the EU.

## Part Three: The Symbols, Country by Country

### Episode 7: The European framework

Before going country by country, two pieces of European background.

First, EU law doesn't ban historical symbols. Council Framework Decision 2008/913/JHA requires member states to criminalise public incitement to violence or hatred, and the public condoning, denial or gross trivialisation of genocide and war crimes. Article 1(2) of that decision lets member states punish only conduct likely to disturb public order or that is threatening, abusive or insulting. This is described in the Polish Criminal Law Codification Commission's opinion on Article 256 of the Polish Criminal Code, discussed below. Symbol bans are national choices.

Second, the European Court of Human Rights has allowed both strict and lenient national approaches.

In *Vajnai v. Hungary*, judgment of 8 July 2008, Hungary had prosecuted a left-wing politician for wearing a five-centimetre red star at a lawful demonstration. The Court found a violation of the right to freedom of expression in Article 10, because a blanket ban on the red star was not "necessary in a democratic society".

In *Nix v. Germany*, decided on 13 March 2018, a German blogger had been fined 120 daily rates of 10 euros for illustrating a blog post with a photo of Heinrich Himmler wearing a swastika armband. He had used it to criticise what he saw as racist treatment of his daughter by a job-centre official. The Court unanimously declared his complaint inadmissible. Germany's history justified its strict line, even against someone whose intent was clearly anti-racist.

So the Convention tolerates Germany being strict. It's less forgiving of blanket bans in countries without that particular history.

### Episode 8: Belgium, our home base

We start at home, because every act of printing and selling happens here.

As far as I can find, Belgium has no criminal ban on Nazi or communist symbols as such. Bills to create one were tabled in the Chamber in 2013 (document 53K2725) and again in 2020 to 2021 (document 55K1586, by Catherine Fonck and others). That second bill would have punished anyone who, "to conduct propaganda or advertising", wears or displays a uniform, symbol or emblem associated with the Nazis. I found no record of either bill being adopted.

What Belgium does have is two content laws. The negationism law of 23 March 1995 punishes anyone who publicly denies, grossly minimises, tries to justify or approves the genocide committed by the German National Socialist regime. The penalty is eight days to one year in prison plus a fine. The anti-racism law of 30 July 1981, Article 20, punishes incitement to hatred or violence.

Neither law is about pictures of symbols. Both are about the message. A neutral historical reproduction, sold as a historical document, doesn't approve of genocide. But a product page that praises Nazi Germany, or a reproduction of an openly antisemitic poster sold as decoration with an approving caption, gets much closer to the line. In Belgium, the framing is where the risk lives.

### Episode 9: Germany, the strictest market we're likely to ship to

Germany needs the most care, so here's the structure.

Section 86a of the German Criminal Code punishes the use of symbols of unconstitutional organisations. Germany's parliamentary research service published a short explainer on this on 30 October 2025, "Aktueller Begriff" number 27/25. It says the offence is an abstract endangerment offence. It is in principle punishable whatever the intent, with the aim of making these symbols a taboo. Germany's Federal Constitutional Court approved that approach on 23 March 2006, in 1 BvR 204/03.

Section 86a(1) number 2 also covers objects. It punishes manufacturing, holding in stock, importing or exporting objects that show such symbols, for distribution or use. So a box of swastika posters shipped into Germany for sale can be an offence in itself.

There are two ways out.

The first is the social-adequacy clause, Section 86a(3) read with Section 86(4). There is no offence if the use serves civic education, defence against unconstitutional efforts, art or science, research or teaching, reporting on current events or history, "or similar purposes". In an article published by Germany's federal media-protection agency, the BzKJ, lawyer Marc Liesching lists the constellations courts have actually decided under "similar purposes": the antiquarian book trade, the auctioning of Nazi militaria to "serious customers", and stamp collecting. That's genuine historical trade. A new reproduction poster sold to the general public as wall decoration is a much weaker fit, and I found no German judgment holding that reproduction-poster retail is covered.

The second way out is opposition. On 15 March 2007, in 3 StR 486/06, the BGH acquitted a mail-order business selling punk products with crossed-out swastikas, or swastikas being stamped on or thrown in a bin. It held that a symbol used in a way that obviously and unambiguously opposes the organisation doesn't fall under the offence. The parliamentary explainer stresses that the bar is high. The opposition must be clear to a viewer "at once". The Bavarian Supreme Court refused protection to a drawing combining the EU flag with a swastika, on 29 November 2023 (202 StRR 88/23), and to a caricature that needed longer reflection, on 7 October 2022 (202 StRR 90/22). A historical poster sold as it was printed expresses no opposition, so this exception doesn't help us.

One more German nuance. Section 86 also punishes distributing propaganda material of unconstitutional organisations. But in 1979, in BGHSt 29, 73, the BGH held that material created before the Federal Republic's constitution existed can't, unchanged, be directed against that constitution. That's why an unchanged reprint of *Mein Kampf* didn't fall under Section 86. The same reasoning plausibly covers original Nazi-era posters, which are also "pre-constitutional". That ruling protects against the propaganda offence in Section 86. It does not protect against the symbol offence in Section 86a. A Nazi poster without any symbol in it is in a better position than one with a swastika.

What about selling from Belgium into Germany online? On 19 August 2014, in 3 StR 88/14, the BGH held that a German who uploaded swastika images to YouTube while in the Czech Republic could not be convicted under Section 86a, because the act happened abroad. Germany closed that gap in 2021 with a new Section 5 number 5a. It now covers symbol use abroad that is accessible in Germany, but only if the offender is German or has their livelihood in Germany. A Belgian company is outside that extension for its website. Physical posters delivered into Germany are a different matter, because the poster then arrives and is distributed in Germany. That's not something I'd test.

The practical German rule: no posters showing the swastika, SS runes or similar symbols shipped to German addresses, unless a German lawyer has approved a specific educational or museum-style channel.

### Episode 10: Austria, stricter than it looks, but with a print exception

Austria has two laws. The Prohibition Act of 1947 punishes activity in the spirit of National Socialism. The Badge Act of 1960 deals with symbols.

The Vienna Chamber of Commerce published a guidance note for second-hand dealers on 24 September 2025, quoting the Badge Act in full. Section 1 forbids badges, emblems, symbols and uniforms of banned organisations, including the NSDAP and the SS, from being worn, displayed, shown or "distributed" in public. Section 3 sets the fine at up to 10,000 euros, or up to 20,000 for a repeat offence, with confiscation of the items. The chamber spells out what this means for dealers: unlike in Germany, selling Nazi memorabilia is forbidden even with the symbol covered, because selling is distributing. Negligence is enough.

But there's an exception that matters for posters. Section 2(1) of the Badge Act says the bans don't apply to printed works, pictorial depictions, stage and film works, and certain exhibitions, "unless the ideas of a banned organisation are approved or propagated". A poster reproduction is a printed work and a pictorial depiction. Read literally, a neutral historical reproduction falls under that exception, provided the presentation doesn't approve of or promote Nazi ideas.

That distinction, objects forbidden but prints allowed if neutral, is my reading of the statute, not a court ruling I found. Also, the Prohibition Act was amended with effect from 1 January 2024, according to the Austrian Justice Ministry's impact assessment. Among other things, the amendment added a provision allowing Nazi memorabilia to be confiscated even without a criminal act, § 3n. Austria is clearly tightening. The cautious line is the same as for Germany: no Nazi-symbol posters to Austrian addresses without local advice.

### Episode 11: France, images online versus objects in public

France has a specific offence. Article R645-1 of the Penal Code punishes wearing or exhibiting in public any uniform, insignia or emblem recalling those of an organisation declared criminal at Nuremberg. There's an exception for a film, show or exhibition with a historical evocation. It's a minor offence, a fifth-class contravention.

This is the law behind the famous Yahoo! case. On 22 May 2000, the Tribunal de grande instance of Paris, ruling on complaints by the anti-racism groups LICRA and UEJF, ordered Yahoo! to take all measures to dissuade and prevent French users from accessing its auctions of Nazi memorabilia. Yahoo! sued in California to have the order declared unenforceable. On 12 January 2006, the Ninth Circuit, sitting en banc, ordered the case dismissed. Eight of eleven judges found jurisdiction, but a majority voted to dismiss on different grounds. So the French order was never tested on its merits in the US.

The most useful recent case for us is from France's highest criminal court. On 5 September 2023, in case 22-85.540, the Cour de cassation dealt with a man who ran a website selling around a hundred historical military items with Nazi emblems, only some of them blurred. The court held that R645-1 requires showing the object itself, ostentatiously, to other people. Photographing items and putting the images online, "even with a view to selling them", is not exhibition under R645-1. The court added a warning: such online display could, in some cases, be the separate crime of apology for crimes against humanity under Article 24 of the 1881 Press Law. Again, the framing decides.

There's one thing to watch. In the 2024 to 2025 session, a cross-party group of French senators tabled a bill, Senate document 624, to make it a crime to offer for sale uniforms, insignia or emblems recalling those of Nuremberg-condemned organisations. The proposed penalty is six months' prison and a 10,000-euro fine, doubled online, with the same film, show and exhibition exception. I found no evidence it has been adopted. If it passes, selling swastika-bearing items to France becomes a real criminal risk.

### Episode 12: Poland, Hungary, the Baltics and Ukraine

East of Germany, the law also covers communist symbols, so Soviet posters matter here too.

Poland's Criminal Code, Article 256, punishes publicly promoting a fascist or other totalitarian state system. Its second paragraph covers producing, importing, buying, storing, possessing, presenting or sending material with such content for distribution. Its third paragraph removes criminal liability when that is done "in the framework of artistic, educational, collecting or scientific activity". In 2011, in case K 11/10, the Constitutional Tribunal struck down the 2010 tightening that had targeted objects carrying fascist, communist or other totalitarian symbols. I'm relying on secondary summaries for the precise scope of that ruling.

Here's the live issue. In November 2024, Poland's Justice Ministry asked the Criminal Law Codification Commission for its opinion on a draft that would turn the third paragraph's exemption into mere mitigation of punishment. The European Commission had argued the exemption was too broad. The Commission's opinion pushed back hard. It said the exemption is compatible with EU law and that the draft would be unconstitutional as far as it touches art, education and science. I found no record of the draft being enacted. For now, selling to collectors in Poland sits inside a statutory exemption. But the word "propagates" still matters, and so does presentation.

Hungary is where *Vajnai* came from. After that judgment and a 2013 Constitutional Court decision, Hungary rewrote its ban, now Section 335 of the Criminal Code, per the MELA project's legislation collection and news reporting at the time. It now punishes distributing or publicly using the swastika, SS insignia, arrow cross, hammer and sickle or red star only in a way capable of disturbing public peace, in particular by violating the dignity of victims. That's narrower than before, but it still covers both Nazi and Soviet imagery.

Lithuania banned public display of Nazi and Soviet symbols in its administrative code in 2008. According to secondary summaries, including Wikipedia's article on bans on communist symbols, collecting, the antiquarian trade and education are exempt. I didn't verify the current article text.

Ukraine is the strictest of all. Law No. 317-VIII of 9 April 2015 condemned both regimes. The Criminal Code's Article 436-1 now punishes producing, distributing and publicly using communist and Nazi symbols, "including in the form of souvenirs", with exceptions listed in the law, such as for museums and scholarship. The Council of Europe's Venice Commission criticised the law. We should simply not ship Soviet or Nazi imagery to Ukraine.

### Episode 13: Italy, Switzerland, the UK and the US

Italy punishes apology of fascism under the Scelba Law of 1952, and incitement to hatred under the Mancino Law of 1993. In April 2024 the Court of Cassation, sitting in joint chambers, held that the fascist salute is only a crime under the Scelba Law where, in the concrete circumstances, it creates a real danger of reconstituting the fascist party. In practice, souvenir shops in Predappio, Mussolini's birthplace, openly sell fascist imagery, as Quartz and the *Christian Science Monitor* have reported. Italian fascist posters are a commercial reality there. The risk again comes from presentation that reads as propaganda.

Switzerland is changing. Today, displaying extremist symbols is only punishable if it is clearly linked to promoting the ideology. On 19 June 2026, the Federal Council adopted its message to parliament for a new special law banning the public use of Nazi symbols, per the Federal Office of Justice and the Algemeiner. Fines would be 200 francs, with exceptions for educational, scientific, cultural and journalistic purposes. It isn't law yet. Watch whether "public use" will be read to include selling online to Swiss customers.

The United Kingdom has no symbol ban. The United States has none either, because of the First Amendment. That's why the Yahoo! dispute was even possible. In both markets the limits are copyright, trademark and the platforms.

## Part Four: Platforms and Payments

### Episode 14: The private rules are stricter than most laws

Here's the uncomfortable truth: in most of our markets, the platforms are stricter than the law.

eBay is the most explicit. Its offensive-material policy, as published today, prohibits "Historical Holocaust-related and Nazi-related items, including reproductions". It also prohibits "Any item that is anti-Semitic or any item from after 1933 that bears a swastika" and "Media identified as Nazi propaganda". It allows historical photos, magazines, books and art as long as they don't "perpetuate or glorify violence, intolerance or racial stereotyping". So Nazi propaganda poster reproductions are out on eBay, while Soviet or American home-front posters are fine.

Etsy forbids items that, in the words of its house rules as quoted by the *Daily Dot*, "support or commemorate current or historical hate groups", naming the Nazi Party. Etsy blocked my direct fetch of its policy page, so I'm relying on that quotation.

Amazon's Offensive and Controversial Materials policy, per its Seller Central help page, bans products that promote or glorify hatred or intolerance. It excludes books, music, video and DVD, and Amazon "reserves the right to make a determination on the historical value of the item". That's discretion, not a safe harbour.

Shopify is the platform we'd most likely run our own shop on. Its acceptable use policy today says you can't use Shopify "to do anything that's illegal where you do business", and can't call for or threaten violence. Shopify removed a broader "hateful content" clause in July 2024, according to press reporting, including BNN Bloomberg in November 2024. In February 2025 it still took down Kanye West's swastika T-shirt store, on the basis of its terms. On Shopify, legality in our own jurisdiction is the floor, but the platform keeps discretion.

Payments matter as much as the storefront. PayPal's acceptable use policy, last updated 29 October 2022, prohibits transactions involving "the promotion of hate, violence, racial or other forms of intolerance that is discriminatory". Selling a Nazi poster as history isn't promoting hate. But the wording leaves room for PayPal to decide otherwise, and a frozen payment account hurts a small shop more than a fine.

## Part Five: Consumer and Product Rules

### Episode 15: Honest labelling and basic product rules

Two quick items that apply to every poster, whatever its subject.

First, say clearly that it's a reproduction. Selling a new print in a way that suggests it's an original is misleading under EU consumer law. It is also exactly the kind of listing that triggers platform takedowns and buyer disputes. Put "reproduction" and the year of printing on the product page, and ideally discreetly on the print itself.

Second, posters are consumer products under the EU General Product Safety Regulation, Regulation (EU) 2023/988, which has applied since 13 December 2024. For a paper print the safety risk is minimal. But the regulation brings paperwork: identifying the manufacturer on the product or packaging, and handling complaints and recalls. Belgium's Federal Public Service Economy publishes the obligations for companies. As a Belgium-based manufacturer we are our own responsible person, which keeps it simple.

## Part Six: What We Can and Cannot Do

### Episode 16: The decision rules

Let's land this. Here are the rules I'd run the shop on.

**What we can do**

- Sell posters whose artist died more than seventy years ago, or which were published anonymously more than seventy years ago, using scans that are free of contractual restrictions. Moor, Deni, Klutsis and Hohlwein are in. Rodchenko joins on 1 January 2027.
- Sell American posters published in 1930 or earlier, and later American posters published without a copyright notice, such as "We Can Do It!".
- Sell British Ministry of Information posters that were published as Crown works, after checking each one wasn't the work of a freelancer who kept the copyright.
- Sell Soviet, Spanish Civil War, American and British posters without symbol worries in Belgium, the Netherlands, France, the UK and the US. Check Poland, Hungary and the Baltics first, where the collector and education exemptions should be confirmed with local counsel before shipping. Never ship them to Ukraine.
- Sell Nazi-era posters from Belgium to buyers in countries without a symbol ban, as historical documents, with neutral, educational descriptions, clearly labelled as reproductions, on our own shop.

**What we cannot do**

- Print artists who are still protected without a licence. Toidze's *The Motherland Calls!* is protected until the end of 2055, and Mjölnir's work until the end of 2050.
- Use scans we downloaded under "non-commercial" terms.
- Use a slogan or design someone has registered as a trademark for our product class without checking the register first.
- Sell Nazi propaganda reproductions on eBay at all, and expect trouble on Etsy and Amazon.
- Ship posters showing the swastika or SS symbols to Germany or Austria without a local lawyer's sign-off on a specific educational channel. In Germany, importing such objects for distribution is itself an offence under Section 86a(1) number 2. In Austria, the print exception holds only if nothing approves of or promotes the ideology.
- Ship Nazi or Soviet imagery to Ukraine, or Nazi-symbol imagery to Switzerland once its new law passes.
- Write product copy that praises, justifies or minimises Nazism or its crimes. That's the line drawn by Belgium's 1995 negationism law, the 1981 anti-racism law, France's apology offence and Austria's Prohibition Act. In every country, presentation is what turns a historical document into propaganda.

**Business judgement, not law**

- Reproductions of openly antisemitic or racist posters, the *Der ewige Jude* kind, are where every risk stacks up: law, platforms, payments and reputation. Even where they're technically legal, I'd leave them out of a shop selling wall decoration. If they're sold at all, it should only be with real historical framing.

Those are the rules. The short version: copyright decides which posters we may print, the symbol laws decide where we may ship them, and the platforms decide where we may sell them. The framing decides whether any of it is legal at all.

Thanks for walking through this one with me.
